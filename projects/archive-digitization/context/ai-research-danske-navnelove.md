## Summary

**Before 1828 — pure patronymics.** Danish commoners had no fixed, inherited family name. A child's "surname" was freshly generated each generation from the father's first name plus *-sen* (son) or *-datter* (daughter) — so a surname reset every generation rather than being inherited. This is directly visible in this family's own line: Peder Christensen → Christen Pedersen → Peder Christensen, cycling back and forth for generations before "Boe" existed as an official surname.

**1828 christening ordinance.** The first attempt at a fix: required every family to freeze whatever patronymic or nickname (*tilnavn*) they were using at that moment into a permanent, hereditary surname going forward. Widely unpopular and unevenly enforced, especially in rural areas.

**1856 circular.** Closed a loophole by banning the adoption of a *new* *-sen* name if a family didn't already bear it — the surname settled at the 1828 ordinance was meant to be final from then on. Enforcement still varied regionally; this family's own records show patronymic cycling continuing well past 1856.

**1904 Name Law (Lov nr. 89 af 22. april 1904).** A genuine name change — as opposed to just freezing an existing patronymic — had required a *kongelig bevilling* (royal grant) since well before 1904, and it was expensive: roughly 33.66 kr in 1875, against a skilled worker's wage of about 26 øre/hour — well over 100 hours' wages, out of reach for an ordinary farming family. The 1904 law kept the royal-grant mechanism as a formality but cut the cost to 4 kr, and explicitly allowed a family to legally adopt a nickname already borne by their father or grandfather as their new official surname. This was partly a response to real administrative chaos: by 1904, roughly a quarter of all Danes were named Jensen, Nielsen, or Hansen, because so many families had simply frozen a generic patronymic at some point rather than adopting a distinct name.

**Why "Boe":** `tree.ged` already documents that "Boe" was in informal local use as a nickname for this specific family as early as **1771** (Jørsby kirkebog) — over 130 years before the 1904/05 grant. The 1904 law's explicit allowance for adopting an existing family nickname as an official surname fits this exactly: the grant formalized an identity the local community had already used for generations, rather than inventing something new.
